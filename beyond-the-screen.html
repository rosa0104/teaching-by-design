Beyond the Screen: Bringing AR and VR Into the Learning Lab

Three AR/VR tools, two areas of focus, and a phased pilot that keeps immersion only where it earns its place.

Teaching by Design · 3 min read
On this page
AR and VR for science Students as creators Inclusive implementation A phased pilot Microblog caption References

When I designed the Flexible Digital Learning & Innovation Lab, I intentionally limited immersive technology to a small set of specialized devices. The goal was never to fill a room with headsets. It was to create a flexible environment where technology earns its place by giving students access to learning experiences that would otherwise be difficult or impossible. My Module 7 proposal extends that idea by recommending three AR/VR tools across two areas: Science/STEM Inquiry and Creativity, Communication, and General Education. Just as important, the proposal keeps conventional videos, physical models, and web simulations in the instructional toolkit when immersion does not add enough value to justify the additional complexity.

For science, I recommend Merge EDU and a small ClassVR/Eduverse station. Merge EDU can place interactive 3D models and simulations into students’ hands, allowing them to examine concepts such as the water cycle, Earth systems, simple machines, or space from multiple perspectives. Research supports this type of situated learning. Chen et al. (2022) found that elementary students using an AR astronomy application demonstrated stronger learning effectiveness, motivation, and flow than students receiving traditional instruction. ClassVR would extend those experiences by allowing small groups to enter environments they cannot physically visit, followed by discussion and application tasks outside the headset.

The third recommendation, Delightex Edu, shifts students from consumers to creators. Using the lab’s Chromebooks and tablets, students can build 3D environments, code interactions, create virtual museums, develop narrated stories, or design models that explain their learning. That is especially important to me because I do not want emerging technology to increase passive screen time. I want students to make decisions, communicate ideas, and create something that demonstrates understanding.

Implementation also has to be inclusive. Szentirmai (2026) emphasizes intuitive onboarding, multimodal information, tolerance for error, and low physical effort in AR design. Stelter and Kim (2023) found generally positive educational perceptions of AR but also identified physical discomfort, including headaches, neck pain, and eye strain, as an important implementation concern. In practice, that means short headset sessions, practice before academic use, seated options, screen-based alternatives, captions and audio supports, and clear learning goals. AR and VR should expand access, not create a new barrier.

My recommendation is therefore a phased pilot, not a technology overhaul. The lab already contains the devices, flexible zones, accessibility supports, and professional learning structure needed to begin. The next step is to use those resources deliberately, evaluate what improves learning, and expand only what proves valuable.
